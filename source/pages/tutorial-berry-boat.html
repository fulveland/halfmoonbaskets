Basket Weaving Tutorial

Willow Berry Boat

A boat of willow knows many voyages. Into the brambles for berries, onto the table for crackers, or tucked on a shelf to cradle tiny treasures.

This is a willow berry boat — a small, shallow basket that is easy to hold in one hand while harvesting.

In this tutorial, I’ll show you one way to approach weaving one. You’ll learn how to shape the form with a simple wooden jig, then weave around the stakes as the berry boat gradually takes shape in your hands.

1. Introduction

This is a willow berry boat. In this tutorial, I’ll show you one way to approach weaving one.

I like these for harvesting because they are easy to hold in one hand. When I’m picking berries, I can place the boat underneath and let the berries fall in as I harvest.

Once the boat is full, it is easy to pour everything into a larger basket or bucket.

I also like using them for more delicate harvests, like fireweed or rosebuds. I can spread things out in a thin layer and let them dry right in the boat. A couple of times a day, I lightly tap the basket on the counter to loosen everything and encourage a bit of airflow.

2. Materials

For this basket, I’m using six thicker willow stakes and about sixteen thinner willow weavers.

The stakes create the shape of the boat, and the weavers wrap around them to build up the sides.

  • Six thicker willow stakes, about sixteen inches long
  • About sixteen thinner willow weavers
  • A wood cookie or cardboard jig
  • Snips
  • Two elastics
  • Two short willow toggles

3. Choosing the Willow

For this basket, especially along the edges, we ask a lot of the willow. The bends are tight, so it is best to use willow that can handle that kind of pressure.

Ideally, you would use willow harvested while dormant and then allowed to dry out just a touch. This is what we often call semi-green willow. Fully dried and re-soaked willow can also work well.

The thin weavers I’m using here were harvested in early spring and kept in my freezer, so they are fresh, or green, willow.

If I have trouble weaving with them, I’ll let them dry for a few days to bring them closer to that semi-green stage.

You may notice tiny leaves beginning to form. That means the willow was harvested just as it was starting to break dormancy. That is okay — it can still be lovely to weave with.

4. Preparing the Stakes

For the thicker stakes, I’m using freshly harvested willow. Because it is summer, the leaves are still on and the bark peels quite easily.

When willow is actively growing, the bark can start to peel off as you work with it. It can get a little ratty looking.

So before I start setting up the basket, I’m going to finish peeling these pieces. If you are using willow harvested in the winter, when the plants are dormant, you can leave the bark on.

5. The Jig

For the shape, I’m using a wood cookie as a jig. The holes are spaced about two fingers apart, which is a good standard spacing for willow work like this.

Because this jig is made from wood, I can only use willow that fits through the holes.

If your willow is a little thicker, or if you are only making one basket and do not want to make a wooden jig, you can make a simple temporary jig out of cardboard.

Just poke holes in the cardboard in whatever shape you want, keeping the holes about two fingers apart.

6. Testing and Cutting the Stakes

I have six thicker stakes for the main ribs of the berry boat. Each one needs to fit through the holes in the jig.

These stakes are freshly harvested and peeled, so they still have moisture in them and are quite flexible.

If your stakes have dried out, that is no problem. Soak them in water for about an hour, and they should become flexible again.

I want these stakes to be as thick as possible while still fitting through the jig holes. When I cut them, I usually measure from the thicker butt end up toward the tip.

If I need to shorten them, I trim away the thinner tip end.

Once I have my six stakes ready, I make a couple of toggles. I cut two short pieces of willow, about two inches long, and wrap an elastic around each one.

These toggles help hold the ends of the basket together while I work.

7. Setting Up the Jig

When I put the stakes through the holes, I alternate the direction of the willow.

One stake goes through tip first, and the next goes through butt first. Then tip first again, and butt first again.

This helps create a more balanced basket because willow naturally changes thickness from one end to the other.

Once the stakes are through the jig, I gather the ends together and wrap them with my toggles.

You do not need to overwrap the elastic. Two wraps is usually enough. I’m using silicone elastics here, but elastics from broccoli or asparagus work really well too.

Once both ends are held together, I choose which side to start weaving on. I wiggle the wood cookie down a little bit to give myself some extra room to work.

8. Choosing the First Weavers

For this size berry boat, I will probably use about sixteen thin weavers.

The thicker parts of the willow are harder to bend, and the thinner tips are much easier to work with.

Because we are starting near the middle of the boat, the weaver has the farthest distance to travel. So I begin with my longest pieces.

To sort them, I tap the butt ends down so they are even. Then I choose from the tallest tips and look for the longest, most flexible weavers.

A little curve or crookedness is fine, as long as it is not right where the willow needs to bend tightly. If a piece feels too thick for the edge work, I set it aside and choose something finer.

9. Beginning to Weave

To begin weaving, I want the butt end of each weaver to rest against one of the two centre ribs.

I alternate sides each time I add a new weaver so the butts do not all build up on the same side.

I start behind the first rib. Then I go in front of the next rib, behind the next, and in front again.

Behind, in front, behind, in front.

The butt end will stick out a little bit. I trim it to about a quarter of an inch. You can trim these as you go, or leave them long and trim them later.

10. Wrapping the Edge

The edge bends are the harder part. I want to wrap the willow twice around the outer rib.

I bring the willow around once, then guide it through. I am not grabbing the very tip and yanking it through. I hold lower down on the willow and gently guide the whole piece around the bend.

I also keep the shape soft and rounded as I pull it through. Then I push the willow tight against the jig and wrap it a second time.

When you are learning to work with willow, it is natural to be very gentle because you are worried about cracking it. But with these tight bends, the willow actually needs support.

Pull firmly, pinch, and guide the willow tightly around the bend. The more firmly you support the willow, the less likely it is to crack.

A little split in the bark is normal, especially with fresh wild willow. If most of the willow is still attached, it is usually fine to keep going.

11. Weaving Back Across

After I wrap twice around the edge, I continue the over-under pattern back across the middle.

If the willow is going under this spoke, then I come back across by going over the next spoke, under, over, under, over, and out the other side.

Then I wrap twice around the outer rib on that side as well.

Weave across the middle, double wrap the edge, then weave back across.

12. Ending a Weaver

As I come to the tip of the willow, I start thinking about how I want to finish this part of the weave.

I finish the weaver the same way I started it — by resting it either on top of a spoke or tucked behind a spoke.

I usually try not to end on one of the outer spokes, and I prefer to tuck the tip behind a spoke if I can.

That means the tips of the weavers finish on the outside of the basket, while the butt ends begin on the inside. But that is just my preference. Your basket will still be strong and useful if you start and end wherever it makes sense.

If I have enough length, I double wrap around the side again and bring the tip back across so it can end neatly in the weaving. If I do not have quite enough length, I might use a single wrap near the end.

13. Adding the Next Weaver

When I add the next weaver, I bring it in from the opposite side.

If the last butt rested on the centre-left rib, this one rests on the centre-right rib.

Again, I start behind the first rib: behind, in front, behind, in front. Then I pinch the butt in so it rests neatly against the centre rib.

From there, I double wrap around the outer edge, weave across the middle, double wrap the other side, and continue.

Technically, you could do single wraps around the sides, but I like the double wrap because it is stronger. It also helps the outer edges keep pace with the weaving in the middle.

If one part of the basket starts climbing faster than another, you can adjust. You might use a few single wraps for a short section, but generally I try to stay with double wraps around the outer edge.

14. A Note on Fresh Willow

The willow I’m using here is our native red willow, which grows all over Alberta.

Once the leaves fall, usually around November, I harvest it and store it outside in a snowbank, or sometimes on the north side of my house.

That keeps it in good condition through the winter. I can bring some inside, let it warm up for about thirty minutes, and then it is ready to weave.

In spring, if there is no snow left, you can also put your willow in a deep freeze.

If you are working with fresh willow and keep getting little breaks, the willow may be too fresh and too full of moisture. If you let it sit for a week or two, it will reach the semi-green stage.

At that point, the tips might feel a little crispy, but the willow can actually become easier to bend.

With fresh willow, little bark breaks can look noticeable because you can still see the green underneath. After a month or so, once the chlorophyll fades, everything tends to soften visually.

You can also tint little pale spots with coffee or tea.

15. Removing the Jig

Once I have about four weavers in place, I usually have enough structure to remove the wood cookie.

I take the elastic off one end and gently pull the jig away.

If the stakes have dried out too much, you can soak them again for half an hour or an hour to make them more supple before removing the jig.

Once the cookie is off, I put the elastic and toggle back on the end to hold the shape. Again, two wraps is plenty.

At this point, I can keep working up the side I started on, or I can turn the basket around and begin filling in the other side.

I like to fill in the middle area as much as possible first. Once there is enough weaving to hold the shape, I can take the toggles off completely and weave between the stakes more easily.

16. Shaping as You Go

Once most of the boat is filled out and I am getting close to the ends, I pause and shape the basket.

I hold it in my hand and see how it feels. I want it to rest comfortably in my palm.

I can make it a little more bowl-shaped, or I can flatten it out and give it more of a long smile shape.

As I get closer to the ends, I use skinnier weavers. They are more flexible and easier to guide through the tight spaces between the stakes.

If I want to bring the ends in tighter, I could move the elastic down, but I find that makes it harder to pass the willow through the gaps. So I usually just hold the stakes in with my hand as I weave.

17. Weaving Near the Ends

Near the ends, the weaving starts to get tight. Some of the stakes may come close together.

When that happens, I might treat two stakes as one. Instead of going over one and under one every time, I might go over two together, then under one, then over one, depending on what the basket needs.

This helps bring everything in tighter and makes the weaving easier when the end of the boat is getting crowded.

If something does not look right, you can always back the willow out and try again.

When I’m near the end, I also think about where I want the tips to finish. If a tip is popping straight up, I might drop the weaver down into a lower row and continue the same pattern there. That helps tie the tip down and keeps the end neater.

Little gaps near the ends are usually okay. I harvest haskaps, raspberries, blueberries, and Saskatoons in these, and they work really well.

18. Trimming the Ends

Once I’m happy with the weaving, I can trim the ends.

I like to think about how the berries will travel out of the basket. If I’m going to pour berries from the boat into a larger vessel, I want the end to feel smooth and open.

That way, the berries can slide out easily.

If you are using the basket more decoratively, or keeping it on a counter, you might leave the ends longer and trim them in a more decorative shape.

19. Optional Lashing

You can also add a lashing at the end if you want.

I do not usually add lashings to my berry boats because when I’m pouring berries out, the lashing can interrupt the natural flow and the berries can scatter more.

But a lashing can be nice if you want to hang the basket on a wall, or if you want the ends to feel more finished and held together.

To add a lashing, I take a thin willow and rest the butt end so it lays along the ends of the spokes on one side of the basket.

Then I wrap around the end, travelling over the butt end and capturing it in place.

I keep wrapping until I get close to the tip, leaving enough willow to finish the lashing without it becoming too flimsy.

To finish, I make a little space between the bottom wrap and the upper wraps. I pass the tip through that space, jumping over one wrap and going under the rest. Then I pull it snug.

The butt comes out one end and the tip comes out the other. Then I trim both ends to about a quarter of an inch.

You can leave one side lashed so it looks finished, and leave the other side unlashed so berries pour out more easily.

20. Final Shaping

Now it is time for the final shaping.

If the willow gets too dry and becomes hard to shape, you can give it another short soak. That will make it more flexible again.

Squish and stretch the sides until you get the shape you want.

Once the boat is fully shaped, I feel more confident trimming the tips and butts a little shorter.

Since I’m weaving with fresh willow rather than soaked willow, it will likely shrink a little as it dries. I still want to make sure the tips and butts stick out about a quarter inch past the stake they are resting on or tucked under.

21. Closing

And there we go — that is a willow berry boat.

Once it is woven, shaped, and trimmed, it is ready for the harvest.
