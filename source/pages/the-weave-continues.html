Caring for your weaving

Your basket will keep changing for a little while after you take it home, and that is part of its life. If we worked with rehydrated materials, keep it somewhere with good airflow while it finishes drying. That takes a week or two, or just a couple of hours if it was made from soft, leafy materials. If we worked with fresh material, it will shrink a little and settle into its shape as it dries over the next two or three weeks.

After that, there is very little to do. About once a year, give it a gentle spray with the hose to rinse off the dust, and let it dry somewhere with good airflow. That is really all it needs.

Sometimes accidents happen. If you ever notice mould starting, mix one tablespoon of bleach into five gallons of water and let the basket soak for ten minutes, no longer. Then set it somewhere warm with good airflow to dry. I like to use the heat vent in my house.

And if your first baskets lean or wobble, keep them close. Those are the ones every weaver treasures most, the ones that hold the learning in their shape.

Harvesting and relationship

Gathering materials is never only practical. It is a conversation with the land, with time and place.

  • In most parts of Alberta, harvesting along roadsides is allowed as long as you stay within eight feet of the road.
  • Confirm the specific rules with your county office.
  • Wear a safety vest. It signals care and authority, and it helps you move safely.

Basketry rewards time spent on the land, paying attention to patterns, to the way plants grow, and to their rhythms. Who does well with regular harvest? When are the seed heads ripe? Who else needs these plants to live? These are a few of the questions worth asking ourselves as we begin to gather.

Materials and care

A few notes on the plants we worked with, and others you may meet next: how to store, soak, dry, and listen to them, along with the seasonal considerations and small acts of care that add up over time.

Willow

Willow bends, roots easily, and grows back after cutting. It has long been used for baskets, fences, cords, bindings, fish traps, caskets, protective charms, and everyday household objects. You will find it growing throughout Alberta, especially at the thresholds between water and land.

When willow is cut back, it does not die. It protects itself by sending up many fast-growing, flexible shoots from the living stool. These are the rods we weave with.

Willow’s ability to heal and regrow comes from its own chemistry. Auxins guide the plant toward new growth, directing where roots and shoots will form, while salicylates help protect damaged tissue from infection and stress. That same chemistry is part of how willow protects us. Inside the bark are salicylates, particularly salicin, the natural precursor to aspirin, long used to ease pain, calm inflammation, and bring down fever.

Identifying and working with willow

Willow grows from a thick butt to a thin tip. Its buds are triangular, with little to no visible scales, and they sit close to the rod, pointing upward to mark the direction of growth. The buds alternate along the rod, offset from one another as they travel around the stem.

Butts of the willow

The butt is where new growth returns from what was already living. It is strong, and it sets the structure.

Tips of the willow

The tip moves through crossings and tight turns. It tends to hold higher auxin levels, higher salicylate concentrations, greater flexibility, and fewer structural lignins.

Harvesting and timing

Harvest willow in winter, from the time the leaves fall until the buds begin to swell in spring. You can weave with freshly harvested willow; store it in a snowbank to keep it frozen, and thaw it for about an hour before weaving. To use dried willow, soak it in a stream or tub. Soaking time depends on size and temperature; thick, long rods may take up to a month, thinner rods about a week. The willow is ready when it swells and the wrinkles begin to disappear. After soaking, wrap it in a damp cloth to mellow for a day.

For willow you intend to peel, wait until the leaves are about an inch long, a sign that the sap is running and the bark will separate more cleanly from the wood.

Willow and the moon

  • New moon. Thought to be the best time to coppice for the health of the stool. Sap flow is lower, cuts place less stress on the stool, and the bark is less likely to peel.
  • Waxing moon. Increasing sap movement. Said to be a good time to cut willow meant for weaving, when the rods are at their most flexible.
  • Full moon. Thought to be the best time for peeling bark. A good time, too, to gather willow tips for willow water, a natural rooting hormone for houseplants.
  • Waning moon. Withdrawal, strengthening, setting. Said to be a good time to cut willow meant for supports, when the rods are at their strongest.

Cattails

  • Harvest when the tips begin to turn brown, usually in early August. Choose plants with only leaves, no seed head.
  • Cut the plant at the base.
  • You can keep harvesting into winter, though the best quality comes early in the season.
  • Split the leaves apart and lay them out to dry, tossing them now and then. When they begin to crisp at the tips, gather them into loose bundles and finish drying in a sheltered, airy place.
  • Once fully dry, bundle them tightly and store somewhere dry until you are ready to use them.

Bulrush

  • Harvest when the seed heads begin to release their seeds, usually early August. Cut at the base and leave the seed heads for the birds.
  • Dry in a hot, well-ventilated place. Drying time depends on the weather.
  • Once fully dry, bundle and store somewhere dry.
  • Only harvest from the same patch every three years.

Preparing cattails and bulrush for weaving

  • Lay down a damp sheet, place the materials on it, and mist thoroughly so the droplets reach between the leaves.
  • Roll them in a damp cloth and let them mellow for about four hours.
  • They should then be ready to weave.
