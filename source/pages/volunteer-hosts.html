Volunteer Hosts

More than one pair
of hands

A good workshop has more than one pair of hands in it. Someone to put the kettle on, welcome people at the door, and notice when a person has gone quiet over their basket.

No weaving experience is needed for most of it. This page is everything worth knowing before you say yes, so you can decide in your own time.

The role

What a host is

A volunteer host tends the room so the weaving can flow. You're the steady, friendly presence that helps people feel welcome, keeps the small things running, and makes sure no one sits stuck and quiet.

You don't need to know how to weave to be wonderful at this. You'll be working on your own project alongside everyone else, with your eyes up now and then.

People often arrive nervous and leave proud. Your warmth in the first hour is what carries them into the second. Holding the nervous part is the whole job.

Teaching

You don't have to teach,
unless you'd like to

There's no expectation that you know the how-to. I do the showing, so you can relax into keeping the room company.

There are many ways to weave the same basket, and yours is welcome here. If you show someone how you do a thing, it's kind to call it your way and say why you like it. That saves them wondering which of us to follow, and most people are relieved to hear there's more than one road.

What to Bring

  • Comfortable clothes you don't mind getting a little plant material on
  • Closed-toe shoes. There are snips and rod ends about
  • A water bottle
  • Lunch or snacks if you're staying through a longer session
  • Your own warmth and patience. The rest we provide

On the day

What you'll do

Yours to tend

  • Welcome people as they arrive, and help them find a seat and settle in
  • Keep tea, water, and snacks topped up through the session
  • Offer reassurance to someone sure they're doing it wrong. They almost never are
  • Watch for anyone stuck but too shy to raise a hand, and flag me over
  • Help someone find their place again in a step I've already taught
  • Hand out waivers and collect the signed ones, keeping them together for me
  • Take photos when the moment feels right
  • Help with a bit of setup before, and tidying after
  • Accept cash for tools and materials

Things you don't need to worry about

  • The teaching, or what comes next. I'll take care of those
  • Fixing or undoing someone's weaving
  • Explaining or vouching for the waiver
  • Anything you're unsure about. Wave me over instead

Card payments. If you're comfortable using a Square reader, let me know in advance and I'll set you up on my Square account so you can help with credit card transactions.

The moment that matters

When someone's stuck

Here's the gentle version.

  1. 01 Reassure first. "That happens to everyone, and it's fixable." Nine times out of ten the worry is bigger than the problem.
  2. 02 Look at the printed steps together. Each project has a printed guide. Helping someone find where they are on the page, and the step they're working on, is often all it takes to get them going again.
  3. 03 Flag me for anything the page doesn't solve. A new step, a real snag, or a question you're unsure about. Catch my eye and I'll come. If I'm mid-lesson with another group, keep them company until I can get there. Company is half the help.

A good check-in

"How are your hands doing?" is often all it takes, and it asks nothing of anyone. An unfinished basket is not a failed basket, and it's worth saying out loud to whoever looks like they need to hear it.

One more thing that makes a difference

Spread out. It's tempting for hosts to cluster together or near me, but the room feels most cared for when we're scattered through it, close to the people working.

Looking after the room

Safety and care

None of this is heavy. It's the handful of things worth knowing in a room full of willow and sharp tools.

What willow does

  • Rods are springy, and they travel further than people expect. Watch for one swinging near a face
  • Long rods reach past the edge of a table and into the walkway behind it
  • When people get focused, tools end up in odd places. Sharp things back on the table, and knife blades folded away after use
  • The floor near the soaking bucket gets wet. Worth drying before someone finds it

Ask before you touch

Some people are glad of a hand on theirs to feel how a movement goes, and some would much rather you didn't. Ask first, every time, and take the answer as it comes.

Snacks and allergies

If someone asks what's in something and you aren't certain, come and find me. It's the one place where a good guess isn't good enough.

If someone is hurt

Stop what's happening in that corner and come and get me. I'll show you where the first aid kit lives when you arrive. If it's serious, call 911 first and find me second. Please don't treat anything beyond what you're trained for. Nobody expects you to.

If someone is unkind

If someone's being unkind, or the room just feels off, you don't have to be the one to sort it. Come and find me.

Packing up

Half a basket looks like scrap to anyone who didn't make it. Please don't throw away anything you're unsure about. Leave it with me.

The waiver

Fielding questions
about the waiver

Handing it out and gathering it back is a real help. This is about what to do when someone has a question.

The one rule

Please don't explain the waiver, or reassure someone about signing it. However kindly meant, a volunteer characterizing a legal document, saying what a clause means or that it's nothing to worry about, can undo the very thing the waiver is there to do.

When a question comes up, your job isn't to answer it. It's to pass it warmly to me. You're not being unhelpful by doing this. You're getting them an accurate answer from the person who can actually give one.

What to say

  • "Good question. Freyja's the one who can speak to that. Let me grab her for you."
  • "I want to make sure you get an accurate answer on that, so let me have Freyja come over before you sign."
  • "That's one for Freyja. One sec, I'll bring her round."

If someone seems nervous about signing rather than asking a specific question, you can offer warmth without touching the document itself: "Take all the time you need to read it, there's no rush at all." Then, if they still have a question, bring it to me.

Best left unsaid

  • It's just a formality.
  • Don't worry about it, everyone signs it.
  • That part doesn't really apply to you.
  • You won't get hurt, it's totally safe.
  • I think that means… (anything interpreting a clause)
  • Even "I'm sure it's fine" is best avoided, because it's a small vouch.

If I'm mid-lesson

It's completely fine for the person to wait a moment. Let them know I'll be right with them, keep them comfortable, and flag me at the first natural pause. A waiver question is worth interrupting for. Please don't let it slide by just because I'm busy.

A quiet note on privacy

The signed waivers hold personal details: emergency contacts, medical notes, allergies. Please gather them and keep them together for me without reading through them. It's simply cleaner for that information to come straight to me.
